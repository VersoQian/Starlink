[Heading 1] 摘 要
[Normal] 商业模式画布是一种包含九个相互关联维度的商业分析工具，广泛用于创业孵化、战略咨询和投资评估。在早期商业分析中，用户最初提供的信息往往并不完整。一个创业者可能只描述了产品想法，却尚未明确目标客户、收入方式、验证路径、关键风险或支撑证据。因此，生成式商业画布系统不应只是一次性生成九宫格文本，还应帮助用户补充缺失信息、查找相关证据，并逐步形成可检查、可修改的分析结果。现有大语言模型虽然能够快速生成商业画布内容，但单次生成方式容易遗漏部分维度，生成结论缺少清晰的证据来源，最终结果也难以按画布单元进行检查和修改。
[Normal] 针对上述问题，本文设计并实现了 Starlink，一个基于多智能体协同的生成式商业画布系统。系统将商业画布生成组织为一个渐进式分析过程。生成前，Ideation Coach通过追问和引导帮助用户补充商业想法中的关键信息；生成过程中，市场、产品、财务等不同智能体围绕各自负责的画布维度进行分析，并结合知识库检索补充证据；共享黑板记录中间状态，使智能体输出、用户决策、引用证据和后续修改能够被追踪。系统还通过监督器检查九个画布维度是否都已生成，并在必要时重新分派缺失维度，从而减少单次大语言模型生成中尾部维度被忽略的问题。
[Normal] 在系统实现上，本文将商业模式画布本身作为主要交互界面。用户可以在每个画布单元中查看生成内容、打开对应引用证据，也可以从证据反向定位引用该证据的画布单元。聊天、引用查看、单元详情、记忆信息和运行状态共同呈现在同一个画布工作区中，使生成结果、支撑证据和系统过程能够被同时检查。实验结果表明，在本文设定的商业画布生成任务中，Starlink相比单次大语言模型生成方式能够更稳定地覆盖九个画布维度，尤其能够缓解尾部维度容易遗漏的问题。检索实验也表明，在嵌入服务降级的情况下，双语混合检索能够保留更多可用证据。
[Normal] 关键词：多智能体大语言模型系统；渐进式画布构建；检索增强生成；商业模式画布；引用溯源
[Heading 1] ABSTRACT
[Normal] The Business Model Canvas is a nine-cell analytical framework widely used in startup incubation, strategic consulting, and venture-investment evaluation. In early-stage business analysis, however, the user's initial input is often incomplete: a founder may describe a product idea without clearly specifying target customers, revenue logic, validation path, key risks, or supporting evidence. Many AI-assisted canvas generators still follow a single-pass pattern, where one model response is expected to produce the full nine-cell canvas. This convenient pattern leaves three structural problems: incomplete information is not actively elicited, generated claims carry no traceable link back to supporting evidence, and the returned canvas is an opaque text block rather than an artifact that can be inspected, revised, and reused cell by cell.
[Normal] This thesis designs and implements Starlink, an end-to-end multi-agent workspace for progressive Business Model Canvas construction. Starlink does not treat canvas generation as one-shot text production. Instead, it organizes the task as a system process in which an Ideation Coach asks targeted follow-up questions, knowledge bases provide external evidence, specialized agents analyze different business dimensions, a shared blackboard records intermediate state, and structured citations connect generated claims to their sources. A supervisor coverage check remains an important structural safeguard in this process: it ensures that the fixed nine-cell canvas is dispatched, checked, and, when necessary, re-dispatched so required dimensions are not lost during multi-agent generation.
[Normal] Based on this design, the thesis completes both the backend and frontend implementation of Starlink, with the canvas itself serving as the primary interaction surface. Users can inspect generated content in each canvas cell, open the corresponding cited evidence, and navigate from an evidence span back to all canvas cells that cite it. Floating chat, citation, cell detail, and runtime status panels are placed in the same canvas workspace, allowing generated content, supporting evidence, and system execution state to be viewed together. Experimental results on the Business Model Canvas generation task show that Starlink covers the nine canvas dimensions more stably, especially mitigating the omission of tail dimensions that can occur in single-pass LLM generation, while improving the inspectability and reusability of the generated artifact.
[Normal] Keywords: LLM-based multi-agent systems; progressive canvas construction; retrieval-augmented generation; business model canvas; citation-grounded workspace

[Heading 1] CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
[Heading 2] 1.1 Background
[Normal] The Business Model Canvas of Osterwalder and Pigneur [1] decomposes a business model into nine interlocking dimensions, and is widely used in early-stage strategy work, startup incubation, and venture-investment evaluation. The combination of a fixed nine-cell structure with open content makes the canvas useful as both a workspace and a presentation medium. Industry retrospectives on startup failures consistently attribute the leading causes to a lack of market need, capital depletion, and competitive displacement, with weaknesses in the business model itself, not just execution, accounting for most of these.
[Normal] In early-stage business analysis, the user’s initial input is often incomplete. A founder may describe a product idea without clearly specifying the target customers, revenue logic, validation path, key risks, or supporting evidence. A generative Business Model Canvas system should therefore produce the nine-cell artifact while also helping users identify missing information, retrieve relevant evidence, and progressively complete the analytical structure.
[Normal] The practical context of this problem is also changing. Generative AI lowers the cost of research, prototyping, content production, and software development, so more early projects can be started by very small teams or even by a single founder. These teams can move quickly, but they often lack separate market, product, finance, and strategy specialists. For such users, the value of a generative business-canvas system is not only faster writing, but also structured assistance in checking assumptions, finding evidence, and turning scattered ideas into a reusable business artifact.
[Normal] Recent language models have made it possible to automate parts of canvas generation. A common implementation pattern for AI-assisted canvas generation sends a templated prompt to a single language model and returns the nine-cell canvas in one response. This pattern is visible at the interaction level in many AI-assisted canvas-generation tools and lightweight prototypes, although the internal implementation of commercial tools is not publicly documented.
[Normal] This pattern, hereafter the single-call paradigm, sends one templated prompt to one model invocation and returns one nine-cell response, with no intermediate state, no resumable checkpoints, and no inspectable handoff between stages. It is convenient but ill-suited to a structured-artifact task. Throughout this work, single-call paradigm, single-pass generation, and the single-LLM baseline refer to the same pattern. Several recurring failure modes follow from the mismatch between this pattern and the canvas’s explicit coverage contract.
[Normal] Three structural problems recur. First, coverage of the nine BMC dimensions is left to the language model's attention during a single forward pass, so less salient dimensions can be omitted. Second, generated claims often have no visible link back to supporting documents, even when retrieval has run internally. Third, the response is returned as one opaque block, leaving little room for dimension-level validation, critique, or user editing without regenerating the canvas. A separate implementation issue is bilingual structured-output instability, such as JSON wrapped in Markdown fences or truncated output. This is handled in the implementation but is not the central research problem.
[Normal] These three failures share a common architectural root. The single-call pattern collapses coverage, evidence tracing, and process visibility into one opaque request-response transaction. Starlink separates these concerns into system mechanisms. Capability bindings and supervisor checks address missing dimensions. Structured citation markers connect claims to evidence or system artifacts. Supervisor-routed dispatch and human-in-the-loop interruption make the generation process visible and revisable.
[Heading 2] 1.2 Purpose and Significance
[Normal] This work designs and implements Starlink, a multi-agent workspace for progressive Business Model Canvas analysis. The system starts from a practical observation: early business ideas are usually under-specified. A useful generative canvas system should fill the nine cells and help users clarify missing assumptions, retrieve supporting evidence, coordinate specialized analysis, and keep the resulting artifact editable and auditable. This is especially relevant for small entrepreneurial teams, where the same person may need to think through customer discovery, product value, revenue logic, risks, and evidence collection at the same time.
[Normal] Starlink responds to this requirement through a staged system process. The Ideation Coach supplements incomplete user input through reflective questions and a seven-step wizard. The knowledge-base layer retrieves evidence from uploaded notes, URLs, and documents. The multi-agent reasoning core assigns different analytical perspectives to specialized agents for market, product, finance, critique, synthesis, reporting, and research. A shared blackboard records the intermediate state of this process, so agent outputs, user decisions, evidence links, and later revisions remain inspectable rather than disappearing inside a single model response.
[Normal] The canvas workspace makes this process usable. Users can inspect generated content cell by cell, open the evidence behind a claim, revise assumptions, and reuse the resulting canvas as a structured artifact. Stage and agent-health indicators show runtime status in the same workspace. The system uses a supervisor coverage check inside this broader workflow as a structural safeguard: it helps prevent the fixed nine-cell Business Model Canvas from being reduced to only the dimensions the model happens to emphasize in one pass.
[Normal] In this framing, coverage is important but not the whole contribution. It is the mechanism that prevents dimension omission during progressive canvas construction. At startup, the capability registry is checked for full nine-dimension coverage; at run time, populated blackboard slots are checked against that registry and missing dimensions can be re-dispatched under a bounded budget. These checks are deterministic and do not depend on the model's self-report, but their role is to support the larger workspace process rather than to replace it.
[Normal] The central research question is whether a multi-agent workspace can make generative Business Model Canvas construction more complete, traceable, and inspectable than a single-call LLM baseline. The evaluation examines this question from several angles: dimension coverage under different model backends, retrieval reliability under embedding-service failure, and the behavior of individual mechanisms such as retrieval grounding, critique, and a limited review loop.
[Normal] The core contribution of this work is the design and implementation of Starlink as an end-to-end multi-agent workspace for generative Business Model Canvas construction. Starlink explicitly represents user input, retrieved evidence, agent outputs, citations, blackboard state, and canvas-level edits, turning canvas generation into an inspectable system process. Guided questioning and knowledge-base retrieval supplement incomplete business information. Specialized agents complete different analytical dimensions. Structured citations and the canvas interface make the resulting artifact traceable and editable. The supervisor coverage check helps prevent the fixed nine-cell structure from losing required dimensions during this process.

[Heading 1] CHAPTER 2: RELATED WORK
[Normal] This chapter reviews six lines of prior work that inform the design of Starlink. To keep the boundary clear, each section first summarizes the relevant literature and then explains how the idea is used in the system. Business decision-support research motivates the canvas task; LLM reasoning and multi-agent systems motivate staged generation; RAG motivates evidence retrieval; interface research motivates the canvas workspace; and observability research motivates the runtime monitoring view.
[Heading 2] 2.1 Evolution of Business Decision-Support Systems
[Normal] Business decision-support systems provide the first foundation for this thesis. Earlier DSS research describes systems that aggregate business data, generate reports, and support managerial decisions [2]. Design-science research further provides the methodological frame for building and evaluating such artifacts [3]. Together, this literature treats an information system as a designed artifact that should be implemented, used, and evaluated rather than only described conceptually.
[Normal] Business-model research explains why the canvas is a suitable target. Teece treats the business model as an object of design in its own right [4], while later work connects AI capability to business-model innovation and feedback loops [5]. Recent Chinese research also discusses generative AI as a force that changes business models and value networks [6]. These works support the view that business-model analysis is not just text generation; it is reasoning about value creation, customer fit, operating assumptions, and evidence.
[Normal] The Business Model Canvas supplies the fixed structure used in this thesis. Osterwalder and Pigneur define the nine dimensions that organize the artifact [1]. Analytics Canvas and Business-to-Analytics Canvas extend this idea by using canvas-like structures as analytical workspaces rather than simple presentation templates [7, 8]. This literature shows that a canvas can serve as a structured medium for analysis, comparison, and stakeholder communication.
[Normal] Explainable-AI research shapes the way generated claims should be presented. Barredo Arrieta et al. frame transparency and interpretability as central requirements for responsible AI systems [9], and Rudin argues that high-stakes decisions should avoid opaque black-box explanations where possible [10]. For business analysis, this implies that generated claims should not be left as unsupported text when users need to justify decisions to managers, investors, or other stakeholders.
[Normal] Existing DSS and business benchmarks clarify the system requirements for this kind of artifact. Many systems focus on dashboards, predefined views, or post-hoc explanation, while benchmarks such as BizBench, FinanceBench, and FinanceReasoning evaluate credibility, comprehensiveness, and reasoning over business documents or questions [11-13]. These works emphasize structured outputs, evidence use, and credible reasoning as important qualities for business-facing AI systems.
[Normal] In Starlink, these ideas are used to define the system target. The generated BMC is treated as a designed artifact with cells, evidence links, user edits, and runtime state. The system should help users reason about business assumptions while keeping those assumptions inside a recognizable canvas structure that can be inspected and reused.

[Heading 2] 2.2 Reasoning in Large Language Models
[Normal] Large language model research provides the second foundation. Transformer models and few-shot prompting make it possible to generate useful business text from a small amount of input [14, 15]. Foundation-model research also gives a caution that is important for this thesis. Bommasani et al. describe foundation models as "critically central yet incomplete" [16]. Broad reusable capability creates leverage, but shared model behavior can also create shared downstream failure modes [16-18].
[Normal] Reasoning research shows that LLM output can be improved by structuring the inference process. Chain-of-Thought, Self-Consistency, Tree-of-Thoughts, Graph-of-Thoughts, ReAct, and Reflexion all introduce intermediate reasoning, search, action, or feedback steps [19-24]. These methods differ in form, but they share a common lesson: complex tasks often benefit from intermediate states and checks before the final answer is accepted.
[Normal] The hallucination and trustworthiness literature explains why these stages are still not enough by themselves. Stronger reasoning does not automatically make outputs grounded, complete, or safe to reuse [25, 26]. For analytical artifacts, fluency alone is not sufficient; users also need evidence, structure, and a way to inspect how the result was produced.
[Normal] In Starlink, these reasoning ideas are used as workflow principles rather than copied as a single prompting template. The system applies staged reasoning to bilingual, structured BMC output, where parsing stability, length control, evidence citation, and nine-cell coverage all matter at the same time. Generator agents produce dimension-level content, the critic checks whether important assumptions conflict across dimensions, and the synthesizer connects related cells into a coherent canvas.
[Heading 2] 2.3 Multi-Agent Large-Language-Model Systems
[Normal] Multi-agent research provides the third foundation. Surveys of LLM-based agents identify role design, planning and orchestration, memory, tool use, application setting, and evaluation as central dimensions [27, 28]. These dimensions describe the architecture of an agent system: what roles exist, what tools they can call, how they share state, how they are routed, how memory is retained, and how the resulting workflow is evaluated. For structured tasks, these questions are more important than the simple count of agents.
[Normal] Existing frameworks illustrate different ways to coordinate LLM agents. CAMEL stabilizes role-playing dialogue, AutoGen provides a tool-integrated conversation framework, MetaGPT uses structured handoffs inspired by software-engineering roles, and Magentic-One demonstrates a central orchestrator that "plans, tracks progress, and re-plans to recover from errors" [29-32]. These frameworks show that multi-agent systems differ in their coordination, tool use, handoff, and recovery mechanisms.
[Normal] Recent commercial agent systems reinforce this direction at the engineering level. Tools such as coding agents, repository assistants, and task-execution agents are increasingly designed to read project context, call tools, run commands, preserve sessions, and expose their work for review. This shows a practical movement from passive chatbots toward agentic workspaces where the model acts through bounded tools and visible state.
[Normal] This engineering trend also clarifies several architectural requirements for agent systems. A useful agent workspace needs a representation of the current task, a set of permitted tools, a place to store intermediate state, and a surface where users can review what happened. Without these parts, an agent may still produce fluent text, but the surrounding application has little control over what was done, which tools were used, or how the result should be revised.

[Normal] Classical multi-agent-systems research adds a useful caution. Coordination quality depends on agreement, convergence, and cost, not just on having many roles [33]. In LLM-agent systems, open-ended conversation can increase latency and complexity without necessarily improving the final artifact. A bounded architecture is therefore often preferable when the task has a known structure and the system needs predictable behavior.
[Normal] Application studies such as Generative Agents show that agent behavior can be organized around roles, memory, and simulated activity [34]. Such studies are useful for understanding how agent state and role behavior can persist across interactions, even when the target application differs from business analysis. They also show why memory should be treated as part of the agent architecture rather than as an afterthought.
[Normal] For Starlink, the useful lesson from this literature is that multi-agent design should make task structure explicit. The system binds agent roles to canvas dimensions, stores outputs in a shared blackboard, and surfaces the result in the canvas interface. The market, product, and finance agents are responsible for different parts of the BMC, while the critic and synthesizer work across those parts. Utility agents handle free-form dialogue, deep research, and report writing without directly mutating canvas slots. This keeps the agent organization tied to the artifact structure instead of becoming an open-ended conversation among agents.
[Heading 2] 2.4 Retrieval-Augmented Generation
[Normal] RAG research provides the fourth foundation. Retrieval-Augmented Generation conditions model output on external documents and reduces reliance on parametric memory [35]. Surveys further distinguish naive, advanced, and modular RAG designs, where retrieval can include query processing, reranking, memory, routing, and task-specific components [36, 37]. This literature frames retrieval as an architectural component rather than a simple prompt attachment.
[IMAGE] media/image9.png
[Normal] Figure 2-4: Four RAG paradigms: naive, advanced, modular, and agentic retrieval-augmented generation.
[Normal] Agentic RAG extends retrieval into a tool that agents can call during a workflow [38]. Dense retrievers such as DPR and ColBERT provide semantic search, while Reciprocal Rank Fusion combines ranked lists without requiring score normalization [39-41]. These techniques support systems in which retrieval can be routed, combined, and adapted to different tasks.

[Normal] For business analysis, retrieval is necessary because useful evidence is current and context-dependent. Annual reports, product documentation, market disclosures, and uploaded company notes cannot be assumed to live inside a model's parameters. This makes evidence retrieval central to AI-assisted business analysis rather than an optional add-on.
[Normal] In Starlink, the retrieval layer follows the modular and agentic RAG direction. Retrieval is called during agent execution, attached to workspace knowledge bases, and connected to generated canvas claims through citations. The key implementation issue is reliability under bilingual and degraded conditions. Dense retrieval provides semantic matching when the embedding service is healthy, while lexical retrieval preserves direct term matches in mixed Chinese-English materials. The two channels are fused so that business evidence can still be retrieved when one channel becomes weak.
[Heading 2] 2.5 Generative-AI Interfaces and Visualization
[Normal] Interface and visualization research provides the fifth foundation. Visual analytics frames the interface as a workspace for reasoning, comparison, and intervention [42]. Human-AI interaction guidelines emphasize clear system capability, efficient correction, and support for recent interaction history [43]. Surveys of generative-AI interfaces further show that complex tasks require more than prompting: users need selection, object manipulation, revision, and intermediate control [44].
[Normal] Business canvas frameworks reinforce this interface choice. The Analytics Canvas and Business-to-Analytics Canvas use canvas-like structures for analysis and stakeholder coordination [7, 8]. Together, this literature suggests that the interface can be part of the analytical process, not just a place where the final answer is displayed.
[Normal] In Starlink, these interface ideas are applied through editable canvas cells, citation markers, detail drawers, HITL conflict handling, and memory panels. The canvas is the final display and the user's workspace for inspecting and revising generated business reasoning.
[Normal] The canvas design in Chapter 4 follows two ideas from this literature. Canvas-style workspaces are well suited to structured sense-making because users can compare, rearrange, and revise intermediate units. Citation markers also make generated text more inspectable by linking claims to evidence. The canvas therefore does more than display the final answer: it lets the user open a cell, inspect its source, compare it with neighboring cells, and revise the content without regenerating the whole artifact.
[Normal] Mixed-initiative interaction research also informs the pre-canvas part of the system. Business ideas often start with missing assumptions, so the system should ask questions before generating the full canvas. This motivates the Ideation Coach and wizard, whose outputs become workspace memory that the main canvas pipeline can read later.
[Heading 2] 2.6 Observability for LLM-Driven Systems
[Normal] Observability research provides the sixth foundation. Production ML systems accumulate hidden technical debt when failures cannot be traced through pipelines [45]. Trustworthy-LLM and agent-evaluation work similarly show that failures in LLM systems need to be attributable to prompts, tools, agents, or trajectories [26, 46]. This literature is especially relevant to multi-agent systems because a final failure may come from a model call, a tool call, a routing decision, or a prior intermediate state.
[Normal] In Starlink, observability is exposed at the level of agents and user actions, in addition to backend service calls. The system therefore records telemetry at three nested granularities: tool calls, agent subgraphs, and user mentions. Tool-level traces help locate slow retrieval, model, or database calls; subgraph-level traces show which agent invocation is affected; and mention-level traces connect the failure back to the user action that triggered it. Chapter 4 turns these signals into the workflow stage strip and the per-agent runtime panel.
[Heading 2] 2.7 Chapter Summary
[Normal] Taken together, these six lines of work explain the design sources of Starlink. DSS and BMC research define the artifact; LLM reasoning motivates staged generation; multi-agent systems motivate role-based coordination; RAG supplies evidence; interface research motivates the canvas workspace; and observability research motivates runtime visibility. The system-specific use of these ideas is then developed in Chapter 3 and Chapter 4, where they become concrete mechanisms for clarifying incomplete input, retrieving evidence, filling the fixed canvas, and supporting user revision.
[Heading 1] CHAPTER 3: PROPOSED METHOD
[Normal] This chapter explains how Starlink builds a Business Model Canvas step by step from incomplete business input. The starting point is practical: the nine BMC dimensions require different kinds of analysis, and a single language-model response can over-focus on obvious dimensions while leaving less salient ones underdeveloped. Starlink therefore treats canvas generation as a coordinated process. Specialized agents analyze different parts of the canvas, their outputs are collected in a shared state, and retrieved evidence is attached to generated claims so users can inspect, revise, and reuse the final canvas.
[Heading 2] 3.1 System Overview
[Normal] Figure 3-1 gives the overall structure of Starlink. The user begins with an incomplete business idea. The system enriches it with questions and retrieved evidence, specialized agents construct the canvas, and the result is displayed as an editable workspace. The reasoning core, retrieval layer, and canvas interface work together in this flow: agents divide the work, retrieval supplies evidence, and the canvas makes the process inspectable and revisable.
[IMAGE] media/image11.png
[Normal] Figure 3-1: Starlink progressive canvas construction framework.
[Normal] The reasoning core divides the canvas task across specialized agents. One supervisor router, three domain generators, one critic, and one synthesizer form the main canvas path. A limited review loop adds three reviewers and one moderator for serious conflicts. Three utility agents handle dialogue, research, and report writing. LangGraph expresses the routing and shared state. Agents communicate through the blackboard, the supervisor dispatches them according to the current state, and checkpoints make intermediate results inspectable. The supervisor coverage check ensures that each required BMC dimension has an assigned agent and can be checked during generation.
[Normal] The retrieval layer supplies evidence for business claims. It ingests, chunks, and indexes documents uploaded to the workspace knowledge base. On each relevant agent invocation, it returns evidence passages through a hybrid search design. The semantic channel uses embeddings to find passages close in meaning to the query. The lexical channel uses direct text matching and is designed for mixed Chinese-English material. The two ranked lists are combined with Reciprocal Rank Fusion. The lexical channel is mainly a reliability measure: when the embedding service degrades, direct lexical matches can still recover useful evidence.
[Normal] The canvas interface presents the generated result as a workspace. Each business dimension appears as an editable canvas cell. Generated claims can include citation markers that let users move from a claim to its source evidence and from a source back to the cells that cite it. Conflicts raised by the critic are shown near the related cells, and agent status is surfaced on the canvas. This lets the user inspect the artifact, the evidence, the revision path, and the running system in one place.
[Normal] On the deployment described in Chapter 4, the end-to-end cycle from user request to first analytical update is designed to be fast once caches are warm, and full canvas generation is expected to complete within a small number of minutes. Precise latency depends on the selected language-model backend and is not the subject of the evaluation. Latency is documented here as an implementation constraint, not as the central empirical claim of the thesis.
[Heading 2] 3.2 Multi-Agent Reasoning Core
[Normal] The need for multiple agents comes from the canvas structure. Customer analysis, value-proposition design, partnership reasoning, and financial modeling use different evidence and judgment. Starlink uses agent roles to keep these perspectives separate, then combines their outputs into one canvas. LangGraph provides the implementation framework. The graph records which agent runs, which state it reads, where it writes the result, and when the next step begins. This supports stable routing, shared state, replayable intermediate results, and resumable execution.
[Normal] The agent roster follows the analytical structure of the Business Model Canvas. The market group covers customer segments, channels, and customer relationships. The product group covers value propositions, key resources, key activities, and key partnerships. The finance group covers revenue streams and cost structure. The critic and synthesizer are needed because the canvas is not just nine independent text fields: choices in one dimension can contradict or depend on choices in another. Utility roles handle free-form dialogue, exploratory research, and structured reporting. Table 3-1 lists every agent role.
[Normal] The agents are not only parallel content generators. They represent different analytical perspectives over the same incomplete business context. Their outputs progressively fill the canvas with assumptions, evidence-supported claims, and relationships across cells. The shared blackboard is central because it lets each agent see the current analytical state while keeping each contribution traceable.

[Heading 3] 3.2.1 Agents and Capabilities
[Normal] As shown in Figure 3-2, the agent topology follows the roster summarized in Table 3-1. This subsection details how each agent's behavior is defined and how the tool registry binds dimension-specific actions to agents.
[IMAGE] media/image14.png
[Normal] Figure 3-2: Reasoning core topology: twelve worker agents under a supervisor router.
[Normal] Model-tier assignments remain part of the agent profile configuration rather than the roster table. Latency-sensitive roles use the base tier, while debate and exploratory paths use reasoning-tier models where deeper deliberation is needed. The critic remains on the base tier because its strict structured-output requirement interacts poorly with reasoning-thinking variants.
[Normal] Each agent's behavior is determined by a configuration that specifies the role name, language model tier, system prompt, bound tools, analytical capabilities, and resource limits. Among these fields, the capabilities list is the load-bearing one. It determines which dimensions of the canvas the agent is responsible for producing and provides the basis for the coverage check detailed in the rest of this subsection.
[Normal] Agents are invoked through the system runtime rather than through ad-hoc direct model calls. Each agent is bound to a subset of the registry with 38 tools, which limits the actions it can perform and the canvas dimensions it can update. Fifteen tools couple one canvas dimension to one of three verbs, analyze, generate, or validate, such as analyze_customer_segments, generate_revenue_streams, and validate_key_partnerships. The remaining tools provide generic primitives for data access, in-prompt analysis, canvas updates, and conflict emission. Figure 3-3 visualizes the bindings.
[IMAGE] media/image15.png
[Normal] Figure 3-3: Tool registry and agent-tool bindings.
[Normal] Every tool in the capability registry is exposed as a typed function with a well-defined input and output schema, so any agent bound to that tool can invoke it without additional integration work.
[Normal] The binding structure has two design consequences. First, because each generation capability is bound into the tools available to an agent at boot time, a missing declared dimension can be detected from the generated state rather than guessed from free-form text. This supports the empirical coverage result reported later: the system can detect and repair missing dimensions, while cell correctness still depends on evidence quality, agent reasoning, and user review. Second, each agent sees only its own bounded set of tools, so the prompt budget for tool schemas remains manageable as the registry grows.
[Normal] Within the progressive construction workflow, the coverage check protects the fixed nine-cell canvas structure while agents supplement information and evidence. It works across startup and runtime: the supervisor checks that every dimension has a responsible generator, then checks populated blackboard slots after each round and re-dispatches missing dimensions under a bounded budget.
[Normal] At startup, the capability registry shown in Table 3-2 defines which agent owns the generate binding for each canvas dimension. This is the static responsibility map used by later checks.
[Normal] After each generation round, the supervisor scans the collected blackboard slots and returns the set of missing dimensions. The check is mechanical and does not depend on the model's self-report.
[Normal] For each missing dimension, the supervisor looks up the responsible agent and adds it back to the next round's active set, along with guidance that names the missing dimension. Re-dispatch is bounded at three rounds.
[Normal] If coverage is still incomplete at the budget, the system records the remaining N-of-9 coverage issue in the run log instead of hiding it or blocking the response. The startup check is strict: the supervisor refuses to launch if any dimension lacks a generator. The runtime repair loop is bounded: it tries to fill missing cells, records the result, and stops. The single-LLM baseline has no comparable mechanism. It can be asked to fill nine cells, but it has no responsibility map or state check that verifies the result after generation.

[Heading 3] 3.2.2 Coordination Mechanism
[Normal] Dividing the work creates a coordination problem: agents must not produce isolated answers that cannot be combined. Starlink solves this by using a shared blackboard. The blackboard can be understood as a structured worksheet. Each agent writes to the fields it is responsible for, and the system checks those fields after each round rather than parsing free-form text to guess what was produced.
[Normal] The blackboard stores the user question, workspace context, current round, agent guidance, retrieved evidence, generated canvas cells, conflicts, cross-cell insights, and graph edges. Domain outputs are merged by identifier so that a revised cell replaces the older version of the same cell without deleting unrelated cells. Control fields such as the current round or supervisor directive use the latest value. This keeps parallel generation safe: market, product, and finance agents can update their own fields without overwriting one another.
[Normal] Definition 1 (merge-by-identity). Merge-by-identity is the reducer used for list-type blackboard fields such as market nodes, product nodes, finance nodes, graph edges, insights, and conflicts. When two lists contain items with the same identifier, the later writer's value replaces the earlier value for that identifier. Items with new identifiers are appended. This reducer makes parallel fan-out safe because the three domain generators can write to their own slots without overwriting unrelated outputs.
[Normal] Algorithm 1: MergeByIdentity
[Normal] Input: oldList, newList
[Normal] Output: merged list with unique item identifiers
[Normal] 1: result <- copy oldList indexed by item.id
[Normal] 2: for each item in newList do
[Normal] 3: result[item.id] <- item
[Normal] 4: end for
[Normal] 5: return result as a list
[Normal] Definition 2 (BlackboardUpdate). BlackboardUpdate is the only state-mutation primitive used by the reasoning core. It receives the current BusinessState, a target slot, and a new value. List-type slots use MergeByIdentity. Scalar control slots, such as current round, supervisor directive, or execution mode, use last-write-wins replacement.
[Normal] Algorithm 2: BlackboardUpdate
[Normal] Input: state, slot, value
[Normal] Output: updated BusinessState
[Normal] 1: if slot is a list-type field then
[Normal] 2: state[slot] <- MergeByIdentity(state[slot], value)
[Normal] 3: else
[Normal] 4: state[slot] <- value
[Normal] 5: end if
[Normal] 6: return state
[Normal] Because every agent output, critic verdict, synthesizer insight, and supervisor directive is routed through BlackboardUpdate, the blackboard transcript remains replayable. Given the same initial state and the same sequence of updates, the final state is identical, which makes the transcript a faithful trace of the run.
[Normal] Definition 3 (SupervisorDispatch). SupervisorDispatch converts the current blackboard state into a routing directive. The directive contains the classified intent, the selected active agents, and the execution mode. Six callability classes are used: standalone-utility, standalone-bmc-generator, standalone-advisor-needs-bmc, standalone-report, debate-side, and debate-judge.
[Normal] Algorithm 3: SupervisorDispatch
[Normal] Input: current BusinessState
[Normal] Output: directive containing intent, activeAgents, and executionMode
[Normal] 1: intent <- classify user intent from state
[Normal] 2: activeAgents <- agents whose callability matches intent and state.round
[Normal] 3: executionMode <- sequence
[Normal] 4: if intent is generate_bmc and state.round is 0 then
[Normal] 5: executionMode <- parallel
[Normal] 6: activeAgents <- all BMC generator agents
[Normal] 7: end if
[Normal] 8: if state contains a high-severity conflict then
[Normal] 9: executionMode <- review
[Normal] 10: add debate-side agents and debate-judge agent
[Normal] 11: end if
[Normal] 12: return { intent, activeAgents, executionMode }
[Normal] For canvas generation, the first round runs the BMC generator agents in parallel. In later rounds, high-severity conflicts add the review agents and moderator to the active set. Figure 3-5 visualizes this dispatch logic as a flowchart.
[IMAGE] media/image17.png
[Normal] Figure 3-5: Supervisor routing flowchart.
[Normal] When the critic flags a high-severity conflict and the debate review loop is enabled in configuration, the system enters the limited review loop shown in Figure 3-6. The loop is capped at three rounds and can be adjusted within a small range configurable at runtime.
[IMAGE] media/image18.png
[Normal] Figure 3-6: One round of the limited review loop.
[Normal] As shown in Figure 3-6, one review round begins when a proponent agent emits a position. The role-specific opponent then challenges it with evidence-grounded counterarguments, and a moderator adjudicates with a structured JSON verdict containing resolved, rationale, revise, position, and confidence fields.
[Normal] In the next supervisor cycle, the responsible generator writes the resolved verdict back into its slot of the shared state. In the observed production trace, a single review round (three opponent calls plus one moderator call) is sufficient to resolve the conflict. The three-round cap is reached only on cases where the moderator returns a non-resolved verdict twice in succession.
[Normal] Observation 1. Bounding the debate at three rounds is an engineering choice, not a purely defensive one: in early prototyping without the bound, contested cases were observed to cycle for many additional rounds with diminishing marginal score improvement past round three. A formal characterisation of the round-by-round decay curve is left to future evaluation work.
[Normal] The limited review loop and the human-in-the-loop modal operate on parallel paths. When the critic raises a high-severity conflict, the server-side review cycle starts immediately. A role-specific opponent challenges the contested claim, and the moderator returns a structured verdict.
[Normal] At the same time, the system emits an interrupt event to the client. The user sees the conflict reason, the critic's suggested repair, and the moderator verdict when it becomes available. The modal offers three choices: accept the verdict, revise it with a user-supplied amendment, or ignore the conflict and mark it resolved.
[Normal] The next supervisor iteration consumes the user's choice as a resume directive. Accept and revise choices write back into the corresponding canvas dimension; ignore stops the critic loop without another revision round. In this design, automatic review produces a candidate verdict, while the user decides whether that verdict should be adopted, changed, or bypassed.
[Normal] The audit log captures human decisions on the same trace as agent decisions. Low-severity conflicts surface as a non-blocking right-rail panel and do not pause the pipeline. This keeps user control explicit while preserving a traceable decision record for contested cells.
[Heading 3] 3.2.3 Output Stability and Provenance
[Normal] Structured output parsing defines the handoff between agent generation and the shared blackboard. In Starlink, an agent response is accepted only when it can be mapped to an expected canvas slot, citation field, or review record. This prevents ambiguous free-form prose from being written into the shared state.
[Normal] Starlink uses three safeguards at this boundary. Schema validated output is requested when the target result is compact and the model provider supports JSON-schema generation. When strict mode is unavailable, a fallback parser strips Markdown code fences and similar wrappers before JSON parsing. Parsed generator outputs are then distilled into compact canvas-facing content, reducing downstream context length and keeping later agents from being dominated by verbose intermediate text.
[Normal] The citation lifecycle makes the generated canvas easier to inspect and verify. In Starlink, a citation is not only a bibliographic reference after generation. It is a system object that connects three elements: the source evidence, the agent output, and the canvas cell where the claim appears. A reader can confirm that a dimension was produced and inspect where each claim comes from. Claims may trace back to retrievable documentary evidence or to a system-internal artifact such as a critic note, a synthesizer insight, or a structural link. The generated artifact is therefore inspectable in two directions: the blackboard slot records that the dimension was filled, and the citation on each claim records the source of what fills it.
[Normal] Table 3-3 lists the four citation classes used in the system, organized by source and user destination. It distinguishes documentary evidence, such as a retrieved knowledge-base chunk, from system-internal artifacts such as a critic conflict note, a synthesizer insight, or a structural link between canvas cells. Each class is rendered as an interactive element on the canvas, so the reader can tell whether a claim comes from a source document or from the system's own reasoning.

[Heading 3] 3.2.4 Memory and Evolution
[Normal] While the blackboard supports coordination within one canvas-generation run, the memory layer extends this coordination across conversations. It carries prior user decisions and completed analytical context forward in time, so the system does not rebuild the analytical context from scratch on every interaction.
[Normal] The shared analytical state holds information for a single conversation. Once that conversation ends, durable memory records distilled conversation summaries, user-approved decisions, and inferred user traits. Memory is what agents consult when the supervisor needs to know which decisions the user has already made or which customer segments the current workspace has already explored. Agents access memory through a dedicated retrieval tool, and the underlying retrieval reuses the bilingual hybrid pipeline described below.
[Normal] The memory model is structured along three orthogonal axes that together determine how an item is stored, recalled, and aged.
[Normal] Memory items are tagged at one of three scopes. Session-scoped items are visible only within one conversation, workspace-scoped items are visible to every member of the same workspace, and user-scoped items follow the user across workspaces. The scope is fixed at write time, but a workspace-scoped item can be promoted to user scope by the consolidation step.
[Normal] Each item also carries a kind label that determines what it represents. The two most common kinds are summary, a distilled conversation or canvas snapshot, and decision, a choice the user has explicitly approved. A third kind, user-skill, records inferred traits about how a particular user works, for example "prefers SMB customers" or "wants risk concerns surfaced early". Two further kinds, insight and constraint, are reserved for the synthesizer and the critic respectively.
[Normal] Importance × confidence. Both axes are numerical scores in 0–1. Importance is set at write time and reflects how strongly the operator believes the item is worth recalling. Confidence is reinforced by re-use and decays with time when the item is not recalled. Retrieval weights the raw similarity score by importance and recency before returning results. Specific threshold values for confidence decay rate, reaping cutoff, and recency half-life are implementation-level parameters configurable per deployment. The values shipped in the reference workspace are documented in the repository's configuration file.
[Normal] The memory lifecycle has three stages. Capture records conversation events such as chat turns, canvas updates, and user accept or reject actions, while deduplicating repeated items from the same trace. Consolidate runs after a conversation and at scheduled intervals. It promotes a finished run into a workspace summary, extracts durable user traits, promotes repeated traits from workspace scope to user scope, and decays traits that have not been reinforced. Reap archives low-confidence items. Archived items remain available for audit but are excluded from retrieval.
[Normal] This three-stage lifecycle is the system's mechanism for evolution. A workspace that has run many conversations builds up a body of user-skill items that influence future supervisor routing and agent prompts. The mutation of memory follows the same update discipline as Algorithm 2. The only difference from a blackboard slot is that memory items survive past the trace boundary.
[Normal] These three properties, namely post-trace survival, write-time-fixed scope, and decay-aware confidence, together extend the blackboard's replay property across conversation boundaries while preventing the unbounded accumulation of low-confidence inferences that typically plagues long-lived agent memories.
[Heading 3] 3.2.5 The Canvas Generation Pipeline
[Normal] Algorithm 4 summarizes the canvas-generation flow. The user request is converted into a shared state containing the question, workspace context, bound knowledge bases, and available memory. The supervisor classifies the intent and selects the active agents. For canvas generation, the market, product, and finance agents generate their assigned dimensions, using retrieved evidence where available. The critic checks the combined result. Serious conflicts can trigger the limited review path. The synthesizer then adds relationships across cells, and the final canvas is persisted and sent to the frontend.
[Normal] Algorithm 4: GenerateCanvas
[Normal] Input: user request, workspace context, bound knowledge bases, memory
[Normal] Output: persisted canvas and frontend update
[Normal] 1: state <- assemble initial BusinessState
[Normal] 2: directive <- SupervisorDispatch(state)
[Normal] 3: if directive.intent is generate_bmc then
[Normal] 4: retrieve evidence for active generator agents
[Normal] 5: run market, product, and finance agents in parallel
[Normal] 6: state <- BlackboardUpdate(state, generated canvas cells)
[Normal] 7: run critic on the combined canvas state
[Normal] 8: if high-severity conflicts exist then
[Normal] 9: run limited review loop and consume user decision if needed
[Normal] 10: state <- BlackboardUpdate(state, review verdict)
[Normal] 11: end if
[Normal] 12: run synthesizer for cross-cell insights
[Normal] 13: persist canvas, citations, conflicts, and trace
[Normal] 14: publish canvas update to frontend
[Normal] 15: end if
[Normal] 16: return state
[Normal] Algorithm 4 has two structural properties worth noting. Every state mutation goes through the update discipline defined in Algorithm 2, which is why the system is replayable from any blackboard snapshot. The only cycle is the review loop at lines 8-11. Outside of it, the execution graph is acyclic, so the supervisor's routing decisions are fully traceable from the run log.
[Heading 2] 3.3 Bilingual Hybrid Retrieval
[Normal] Generated business claims need evidence, especially when users upload company notes, market reports, product documents, or URLs. Starlink retrieves relevant passages before agent generation and exposes them as citations in the generated cells. The retrieval design combines semantic search with direct lexical matching for mixed Chinese-English material. Reciprocal Rank Fusion merges the two ranked lists. When the embedding service is healthy, semantic retrieval is already strong. The lexical channel mainly improves reliability when embedding calls degrade.
[IMAGE] media/image22.png
[Normal] Figure 3-8: Hybrid retrieval pipeline.
[Heading 3] 3.3.1 Pipeline and Ingestion
[Normal] Four ingestion paths are supported: text seed, URL fetch, file upload, and administrative SQL. The URL path is guarded against SSRF and DNS-rebinding. The chunker is paragraph-first with a 600-character target and 80-character overlap. These values were chosen by manual inspection on the deployment corpus. Downstream retrieval quality on the §3.4.2 evaluation set remained stable when each parameter was independently varied by roughly half: target from 300 to 900 characters, and overlap from 40 to 120 characters.
[Normal] The retrieval layer supports three remote embedding providers and a deterministic local fallback, listed in Table 3-4. The fallback is used only when the remote provider fails. It uses the same Unicode-class tokenization as the lexical channel, builds a sparse 1 536-dimensional bag-of-tokens vector, and L2-normalizes it so cosine similarity remains well defined. Its quality is, by design, far below any real embedding model; its role is availability, not quality.

[Heading 3] 3.3.2 Bilingual Tokenization and Rank Fusion
[Normal] The lexical tokenizer supports bilingual matching without language detection. It keeps Latin words only when they are at least three characters long, which removes many common English stopwords without a separate stopword list. It segments Chinese text as bigrams, because single characters are often too frequent to discriminate well. It also restricts matching to the common CJK ideograph range. This keeps the lexical channel simple while still covering the target corpus.
[Normal] A whitespace-and-lowercase tokenizer fails entirely on Chinese, and a dictionary-based Chinese segmenter fails on English. The Unicode-class-based hybrid runs in a single linear pass with no language-detection step and produces a deduplicated set of length-bounded tokens. The resulting tokens are matched against the stored chunk content via a substring-match query. No additional database extension is required, and the lexical channel works on any database that already supports vector retrieval.
[Normal] The dense and lexical rankers are fused under Reciprocal Rank Fusion, originally proposed by Cormack, Clarke, and Büttcher [41]:
[Normal] k = 60 is the canonical default, with smaller k sharpening top-rank weighting. RRF was chosen over weighted sum for two reasons. Cosine similarity and occupy incomparable scales, so weighted sum would require both score normalization and a hyperparameter for the weight, neither of which is portable across corpora. RRF is also normalization-free and gracefully tolerates single-ranker failure: when one ranker returns nothing, the other dominates the fused ranking without special-case code.
[Normal] A post-filter applies to the cosine component: . The disjunction is deliberate. The first clause is the precision floor under normal operation. The second clause is the safety net, a chunk with weak semantic match but strong lexical signal survives. That second clause is precisely what matters under embedding-API degradation.
[Normal] Algorithm 5 specifies the end-to-end retrieval procedure for a single query, combining the design choices above.
[Normal] Algorithm 5: HybridRetrieve
[Normal] Input: query, bound knowledge bases, topK
[Normal] Output: filtered evidence chunks with fused ranking
[Normal] 1: denseResults <- vectorSearch(query, bound knowledge bases)
[Normal] 2: denseRanks <- rank by cosine similarity
[Normal] 3: lexicalTokens <- tokenize with CJK bigrams and Latin terms
[Normal] 4: lexicalResults <- substringMatch(lexicalTokens, bound knowledge bases)
[Normal] 5: lexicalRanks <- rank by lexical hit strength
[Normal] 6: fused <- reciprocalRankFusion(denseRanks, lexicalRanks)
[Normal] 7: filtered <- chunks where cosine >= 0.55 or lexical_hits >= 2
[Normal] 8: return topK chunks from filtered fused ranking
[Normal] In Algorithm 5, steps 1 and 2 are the standard dense retrieval pipeline. Steps 3 and 4 add the Unicode-class lexical channel for bilingual input. Steps 5 and 6 apply the standard RRF algorithm of Cormack et al. [41]. Step 7 is a post-filter that keeps a chunk if either ranker considers it strong, and step 8 returns the final top-K evidence chunks. With a healthy embedding service, the dense channel dominates. Under jitter, the embedding call returns degraded local-hash vectors, the dense channel weakens, and the lexical clause carries most of the recall.
[Heading 3] 3.3.3 Agent–KB Binding and Execution Trace
[Normal] Knowledge bases are not silently consumed by every agent. Each KB is explicitly bound to one or more target agents, and the relation is many-to-many: a compliance-reference KB may be shared by both critic and product-agent, and the deep-research agent may carry both an industry-trends KB and a workspace knowledge base containing private company memory.
[Normal] Each binding has an auto_search flag that distinguishes two modes. When the flag is enabled, the runtime performs hybrid retrieval against the KB on every invocation of the bound agent and prepends the top-K chunks to the agent's context. When the flag is disabled, the binding remains available but opt-in: the agent can still reference the KB through the knowledge-base tool, but no automatic pull happens. This matches the user's expectation that binding a KB to an agent should make the agent use it when needed.
[Normal] To prevent prompt context from growing without bound, the mention router caps the evidence budget per invocation at about three thousand characters. The budget is computed as five bound KBs, three top chunks, and about two hundred characters per chunk. This guard keeps retrieval useful without letting evidence text consume the whole model context.
[Normal] A single canvas generation exercises the full pipeline. The supervisor classifies the prompt as a canvas-generation intent and dispatches the three domain generators in parallel. Each generator retrieves from its bound knowledge bases and emits its assigned dimensions with citation tokens. The critic reads the outputs and returns severity-classified conflicts. Low-severity conflicts become advisory markers, while high-severity conflicts can trigger limited review. The synthesizer then adds relationships across cells. Finally, the canvas is persisted and pushed to the frontend through the subscription channel.
[Heading 2] 3.4 Experiments and Analysis
[Normal] The experiments focus on the measurable back-end mechanisms of the system, especially retrieval reliability and dimensional coverage. The user-facing progressive workflow is demonstrated through the case walkthrough in Chapter 4.
[Heading 3] 3.4.1 Experimental Setup
[Normal] Methodologically, the evaluation follows the design-science research methodology of Hevner et al. [3] and the procedural articulation by Peffers et al. [47]: the implemented Starlink workspace serves as the designed artifact. The controlled comparison against a single-LLM baseline constitutes the demonstration. The rubric-based Agent-as-Judge scoring of Zhuge et al. [48] constitutes the evaluation. Case selection follows the purposive-sampling principle of Yin's case-study methodology [49], with the twelve YC seed-stage cases chosen for theoretical relevance to early-stage canvas reasoning, not statistical representativeness.
[Normal] The reasoning core uses different language-model tiers for different paths. A base-tier model serves latency-sensitive generator, supervisor, and synthesizer calls. A reasoning-tier model serves review and utility paths, while a stronger reasoning variant is reserved for deep research. Exact model identifiers are recorded in the raw benchmark reports. Embeddings use a 1 536-dimensional commercial provider with a deterministic local-hash fallback for jitter tests. Canvases, citations, agent traces, and judge scores are persisted in a relational database with vector search support.
[Normal] The single-LLM baseline, referred to as gpt-solo in raw reports, sends one templated prompt to one language model and parses the returned nine-cell canvas. It carries no retrieval, no agent decomposition, and no inter-step state, matching the single-pass configuration discussed in Chapter 1. The mechanism-ablation variants minimal, no-RAG, no-critic, no-debate, and full selectively toggle retrieval grounding, the critic, and the limited review loop within the multi-agent system. The retrieval evaluation has its own pure-vector baseline run under healthy and jitter-injected regimes.
[Normal] The headline evaluation uses twelve YC seed-stage cases: Stripe, Airbnb, Replit, Pebble, Coursera, Notion, Coinbase, DoorDash, Twitch, Segment, Brex, and Substack. Each case contains a business question, a small company knowledge base, and a ground-truth BMC built by the author from public materials such as founder interviews, pitch decks, filings, product documents, and major reporting. Each dimension contains three to seven must-cover concepts. When sources conflicted, the most recent or authoritative source was preferred. Six cases form the validator subset for ablation. The retrieval evaluation uses three knowledge bases and twenty BMC queries from the same corpus.
[Normal] The evaluation adopts the Agent-as-Judge protocol of Zhuge et al. 2024 [48] with strict structured output. The same judge, deepseek-flash v4, is used for every run. It comes from a model family distinct from the systems under test to reduce same-family scoring bias. Each BMC dimension is scored from 0 to 3: absent or wrong, partial coverage, full coverage with plausible specifics, and defensible non-obvious nuance. A canvas can therefore receive 0 to 27 points. Because the judging path is fixed, score differences come from the system outputs rather than judge changes.
[Normal] The judge uses one fixed prompt template for every system condition and case. It receives only the generated canvas content, with system identifiers such as agent name, model tier, and conversation ID removed. Before live runs, the judge was calibrated against three weak inputs: a question echo, an empty JSON object, and generic BMC boilerplate. All three received near-floor scores in the 0-2 range. This indicates that the rubric rewards must-cover concept presence rather than text length or surface polish. The raw judge logs preserve the calibration outputs.
[Heading 3] 3.4.2 Retrieval Quality and Embedding-Service Jitter
[Normal] The retrieval evaluation uses three knowledge bases, one for each BMC semantic family, and twenty BMC queries from the Chapter 4 deployment corpus. The queries were derived from the nine canonical BMC dimensions across five companies, then deduplicated. The author marked relevant chunks through a full reading of the indexed corpus. Recall@5 measures how many marked chunks appear in the top five results. The bilingual hybrid retriever is compared with a pure-vector baseline under healthy embeddings and under a stress test where embeddings are forced onto the deterministic local-hash fallback.
[Normal] Under that stress test, the hybrid retriever recovers +28.4% recall@5 over the pure-vector baseline, as shown in Table 3-5. Graded partial-failure jitter, for example 30% / 50% / 80% API error rates, is out of scope here and listed in Section 5.2 as future evaluation work.
[Normal] Under healthy operation, the dense channel saturates. Top-1 retrieval is correct on every query, with MRR = 1.0. The lexical channel has no statistical room to add signal, so vector and hybrid are indistinguishable on recall@5, precision@5, and MRR. As shown in Table 3-5, the contrast appears once the embedding service is forced onto its deterministic local-hash fallback.

The expectation is that retrieval grounding should most affect must-cover concept presence, because the rubric rewards specific factual concepts. The critic and review loop target conflict suppression, so removing them should mainly affect worst-case consistency rather than the mean. Table 3-7 follows this pattern.

The shared blackboard collects their outputs and makes missing dimensions visible. The retrieval layer attaches evidence to generated claims and remains useful when the embedding service degrades. [Normal] The Ideation Coach is responsible for the earlier stage of business analysis: helping the user articulate a viable idea before any canvas dimension is generated. [Heading 3] 4.3.7 Utility Agents Beyond the Canvas
[Normal] Band 5 of Table 3-1 lists three utility agents that share the LangGraph topology with the canvas pipeline but do not write into canvas slots. [Heading 2] 4.4 Design Mechanisms
[Normal] The pipeline stages above rely on a set of cross-cutting design mechanisms: the visual identity, the dual-mode layout, the streaming merge under load, the layered floating UI, the citation click path, and the human-in-the-loop conflict decision. This section pulls these out of any single stage and treats them as design-level properties.
[Heading 3] 4.4.1 Four Design Tensions
[Normal] Four design tensions surfaced during early prototyping. The design responses appear throughout this section.
[Normal] Structured semantics versus free arrangement. The canvas is a fixed nine-cell semantic structure, but users prefer to arrange cards freely, for example, to compare several candidate value propositions side by side.
[Normal] Many agents writing into one viewport. Multiple agents emit content into the canvas concurrently. Automatic layout must keep their output legible without erasing arrangements the user has already made.
[Normal] Streaming layout stability. Streaming generation must not trigger a full re-layout on every incremental update. Updates must complete within a single frame budget.
[Normal] Layered floating UI and interruption. Cells, edges, citation chips, drawers, the chat dock, modals, and the health indicator all coexist on a single viewport. Stacking order and anti-overlap rules must be deterministic, especially when a human-in-the-loop conflict modal pauses the workflow and asks the user to make a decision.
[Heading 3] 4.4.2 Editorial Boardroom Design Language
[Normal] Visual identity is intentionally distinct from the dominant AI-product aesthetic: a strategic-consulting boardroom, not a chat interface. A small set of design tokens realises the system: a serif display face, a humanist sans body, a monospace face for numbers and identifiers, and an ink-on-paper palette with five tonal steps plus a single saturated accent. The result reads more like a print page than a chat panel. Reserved for moments that genuinely demand the user's attention (a high-severity critic conflict, a human-in-the-loop blocking prompt), the single saturated accent appears sparingly.
[Heading 3] 4.4.3 Dual-Mode Canvas and Visual Primitives
[Normal] Two view modes share a single viewport: a freeform infinite canvas for divergent exploration, and a strict nine-cell grid for structured presentation. The same nodes are shared between modes. The toggle just swaps the layout strategy. In informal user observations, the freeform mode is preferred during exploration, the grid mode when presenting finished work.
[IMAGE] media/image44.png
[Normal] Figure 4-17: Dual-mode canvas on a single workspace.
[Normal] The visual primitive set consists of nine node types: one per BMC cell, plus agent-avatar, insight-note, plan-node, canvas-note, data-source, report-card, conflict-alert, and canvas-image; and five edge classes: structural, conflict, suggestion, dependency, and data-flow. Each class carries its own visual treatment, so a reader can identify the nature of any connection on the canvas without selecting it.
[Heading 3] 4.4.4 Streaming Incremental Merge and Floating UI
[Normal] The performance-critical path for streaming updates is an incremental merge that combines incoming nodes with the current viewport state by identity. The merge completes within a single frame budget on viewports holding a typical canvas of a few hundred to a thousand nodes. A defensive variant protects against a transient subscription delivering an inconsistent snapshot: when an incoming snapshot has fewer nodes than the current state, it is treated as a union, not as a replacement.
[Normal] Eight floating components share the viewport: the chat dock, the citation panel, the per-cell detail drawer shown in Figure 4-12, the ideation coach shown in Figure 4-8, the agent-health indicator, the action bar, the knowledge-upload modal shown in Figure 4-7, and the evidence drawer. Three anti-overlap mechanisms keep the viewport readable. When the citation panel opens, the coach column shifts to make room. When the viewport falls below a desktop-width threshold, the chat dock and the citation panel become mutually exclusive. Stacking order is layered so that critic-flagged conflict edges always float above the cells they relate.
[Heading 3] 4.4.5 Citation as Interaction
[Normal] A citation that an agent emits inline as a typed token is parsed into structured metadata, persisted with the canvas node, rendered as a clickable chip, and resolved on click back to the original passage in an evidence drawer. A defensive fallback covers the case where the local evidence cache misses, falling back to a live database lookup instead of showing an "evidence unavailable" placeholder. A reverse-lookup query closes the audit loop in the other direction: from any cited passage, the user can list every canvas node that cites it.
[Normal] The same citation token is therefore a single artifact threaded through the entire system: it is emitted, parsed, persisted, rendered, and then clicked. The important requirement is that this thread remains connected from source evidence to canvas claim. The canvas itself is where the user inspects that connection, so no separate explanation panel is needed.
[Heading 2] 4.5 Runtime Observability
[Normal] Observability extends the progressive canvas process into runtime operation. It shows whether the agents, tools, and user interactions that support canvas generation are running normally, slowing down, or failing. The monitoring view is organized around three telemetry axes and two canvas-level views: the workflow stage strip and the per-agent runtime panel. Together, they let users and developers see phase and agent state without opening backend logs.
[Heading 3] 4.5.1 Three-Layer Monitoring View
[Normal] Every declared capability has a corresponding telemetry stream at the tool layer. Every agent that holds such a capability has a corresponding stream at the subgraph layer. Every user interaction that should exercise a set of declared capabilities has a corresponding stream at the mention layer. If a capability is not exercised when expected, the missing activity becomes visible in one of these layers.

[Normal] A five-stage strip at the top of the canvas shows the current pipeline phase: input, generate, review, synthesize, or report. Each stage carries a count badge from the current blackboard state. For example, generate shows nine cells after the three generators have written, and report becomes non-zero after the user invokes the report-writer. [Normal] The agent-health indicator reports which agent's tools are slow or failing. The per-agent runtime panel answers a related question: what each agent contributed to the canvas. It shows per-agent node counts, contribution timelines, and the workflow stage in which each output appeared. Together, these views show both liveness, such as latency and errors, and contribution, such as generated nodes and stages. This gives the user a drill-down path without leaving the canvas.

[Heading 2] 5.1 Conclusion
[Normal] Starlink, designed and implemented in this work, is an end-to-end multi-agent workspace for progressive Business Model Canvas construction. Twelve specialized agents are organized under a supervisor-routed shared-blackboard architecture. The system is supported by bilingual hybrid retrieval, a limited review loop for serious cross-dimension conflicts, and a three-layer monitoring view. A supervisor coverage check remains an important mechanism inside this process. It checks whether the fixed nine-cell canvas has been produced, validates populated blackboard slots after each round, and re-dispatches missing dimensions under a bounded budget. Its role is to detect and repair missing dimensions, not to certify the correctness of every generated claim.
[Normal] Experimental evaluation supports the design rather than defining it. On the strong DeepSeek-V3 backend, the single-LLM baseline leaves the partnership dimension empty in all twelve cases, while Starlink fills it in every completed run. Aggregate rubric scores are close on this backend, with single-LLM scoring 20.2 and Starlink scoring 20.8.
[Normal] On the mid-capability MiniMax-M2.5 backend, the difference widens to +6.8 points and eight of twelve case-wise wins for Starlink. This is consistent with the interpretation that supervisor coverage checks help more when the underlying model under-allocates attention to less salient BMC dimensions. Under embedding-service jitter, the bilingual hybrid retriever recovers most of the lost recall, supporting its role as a reliability mechanism.
[Normal] Several limitations of this work are also acknowledged. Streaming is section-level, not token-level. Observability-window state is process-local in multi-replica deployments. Multi-tenant isolation relies on database-level row-level security but does not include row-level encryption. Case corpus is narrow, drawn primarily from software, consumer SaaS, and fintech. User study is small. Two threats to validity apply: a potential judge bias toward verbose outputs, and single-judge evaluation without human-agreement κ on a subsample. The empirical scope is restricted to one strong and one mid-capability backend. The coverage pattern reproduces on both, but broader cross-family replication and sub-frontier capability characterization remain future work.
[Heading 2] 5.2 Future Work
[Normal] Future work should extend Starlink in two directions: stronger evaluation and more mature system capability.
[Normal] On the evaluation side, graded partial-failure jitter testing would make the retrieval stress test more realistic. Instead of only testing complete dense-channel collapse, future experiments could introduce 30%, 50%, or 80% embedding-request failure rates. This would show how the bilingual hybrid retriever degrades under production-like instability.
[Normal] A conflict-injection benchmark would also be valuable. The current must-cover rubric measures whether all canvas dimensions are produced, but it does not directly test whether the critic and limited review loop can detect contradictions across dimensions. Future cases could deliberately plant conflicts, such as a revenue model that contradicts the customer segment or a pricing strategy that conflicts with the channel design. This would make the review loop itself measurable.
[Normal] Cross-LLM-family replication is another necessary step. The current experiments cover DeepSeek-V3 and MiniMax-M2.5. Running the same benchmark on Claude, GPT, Gemini, and Llama-family models would clarify whether the Key Partnerships omission pattern is specific to the tested models, related to model capability, or more general to single-LLM structured generation.
[Normal] On the system side, token-level streaming would reduce the perceptual delay during long generations. This would require an incremental citation parser, because citations must remain valid even while the output is still being generated.
[Normal] The retrieval layer can also be extended beyond Chinese and English. The current lexical design is suitable for Chinese-English retrieval, but other CJK scripts and Latin-script languages would require separate segmentation and token-length tuning.
[Normal] Online agent reconfiguration is another practical direction. Prompt and capability edits should eventually take effect without restarting the server, which would make the system easier to use as a research and prototyping workspace.
[Normal] A further direction is distillation through preference-based fine-tuning. The current multi-agent ensemble improves coverage and structure, but it also increases latency and operational cost. Using judge scores or human preferences as training signals may allow part of the orchestration behavior to be compressed into a smaller specialist model.
[Normal] The evaluation corpus should be expanded beyond software, SaaS, and fintech. Cases from hardware, direct-to-consumer retail, B2B enterprise, deeptech, and manufacturing would test whether the dimension-coverage blind spot remains stable across different business domains.
[Normal] Taken together, these directions would strengthen Starlink's empirical validity and system maturity. The next step is to examine where the supervisor coverage check works, where it fails, and whether its advantage remains stable across models, domains, and deployment settings.
